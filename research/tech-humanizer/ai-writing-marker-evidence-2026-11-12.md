# Research: Evidence behind the tech-humanizer AI-writing markers

Date: 2026-11-12. Subject: Which primary-source evidence supports or contradicts the AI-writing markers that `tech-humanizer` uses, and where the skill's own wording goes beyond that evidence. Context: The candidate skill rewrites technical documents, emails and chat messages to remove AI-shaped prose. Its marker references name one editorial source and no measurement. The note feeds a decision on which claims to keep, soften or tag in [the skill references](../../incubator/tech-humanizer/candidate/tech-humanizer/references/ai-markers.md). Confidence markers:

- **[official]**: the relevant part of official docs, a specification, a tool's README, or a paper page was read.
- **[official · summary only]**: only an abstract or overview was read, or the page does not cover the detail.
- **[secondhand]**: a third-party description, such as a search result summary; not traced to a primary source.
- **[inference]**: the author's judgment, with no direct evidence.
- **[measured]**: the result of a command run on the stated date (here, 2026-11-12).
- **unverified** (note-specific): the owning source could not be opened, or a claim on a page that was read was not traced to its source.

## 3. Reliability of AI-text detection and marker-based judgement

### Evidence

**Automated detectors.** These studies tested perplexity-based and supervised detectors, not marker lists. The link to marker density is my inference.

- Liang et al., arXiv 2304.02819: seven detectors on 91 TOEFL essays and 88 US eighth-grade essays. Average false-positive rate on TOEFL essays was 61.22%; 18 of 91 (19.78%) were flagged by all seven and 89 of 91 (97.80%) by at least one. Asking ChatGPT to "enhance the word choices to sound more like that of a native speaker" cut the average false-positive rate to 11.77%. Asking for more literary language reduced detection of 31 ChatGPT-3.5 Common App essays, and a technical-language prompt cut detection of 145 generated abstracts from up to 68% to up to 28%. Limits stated by the authors: small samples, mostly GPT-2-backbone detectors, a focus on perplexity and supervised methods. [official]
- Sadasivan et al., arXiv 2303.11156: recursive paraphrasing of passages of about 300 tokens dropped true-positive rate at 1% false-positive rate from 99.3% to 9.7%, and zero-shot detector AUROC from 96.5% to 25.2%. Human text can be spoofed into detection. The paper ties best-possible AUROC to the total-variation distance between human and AI text, so better models make detection harder. [official]
- Weber-Wulff et al., arXiv 2306.15666: 12 public tools plus Turnitin and PlagiarismCheck; "all scored below 80% of accuracy and only 5 over 70%". Machine-translated human text lost 20 points of accuracy. Machine paraphrase of AI text gave 26% accuracy. The authors conclude the tools "should not be used in academic settings". Their document set is limited and does not include hybrid human-AI writing. [official]
- Dugan et al. (RAID), arXiv 2405.07940: abstract only. Over 6 million generations, 11 models, 8 domains, 11 attacks; detectors are "easily fooled" by attacks, sampling changes and unseen models. [official · summary only]

**Human and marker-based judgement.** Russell et al. is the closest test of people using vocabulary and structure cues. Annotators who frequently use LLMs for writing: individual false-positive rate about 3.3% and true-positive rate about 92.7%; the five-expert majority missed 1 of 300 articles. Four annotators with limited LLM experience: about 56.7% true-positive and 52.5% false-positive rate. Nonexperts "take the inclusion of any 'fancy' or otherwise low-frequency word types as signs of AI", believe humans write more grammatical sentences than AI does, and "attribute any text written in a neutral tone to AI, resulting in many false positives". Limits: American English, edited non-fiction articles from 2024 models. [official] The Wikipedia page repeats the warning: "Do not rely too much on your own judgment" and "Detecting LLM texts on the basis of style alone is not as easy as it seems"; it also says formal or fancy prose, perfect grammar and isolated transition words are ineffective indicators. [official] Two studies it cites for weak human accuracy were not opened (a 2025 Advances in Simulation article and a 2025 German-theses article). [unverified]

**Non-native writers.** The measured evidence is for detectors (Liang, Weber-Wulff machine translation), not for marker lists. Kobak and Kousha both note that non-native authors may use LLMs for editing, and Kobak adds that native speakers may be better at removing style words. The Wikipedia revision contains no mention of non-native writers as a false-positive group; its one note is that non-native editors may avoid repeating words (relevant to C6). A marker that targets formal vocabulary (L6) or a list of "fancy" words (L1) would fall most heavily on the writers these studies identify. That last step is inference. [inference]

**Newer models.** Juzek and Ward's GPT-4o-mini appendix shows word-level change between versions. The Wikipedia page's era table and its section on differences between models say vocabulary and style vary by model; it states that GPT-4o differs more from human writing than other contemporaneous models, citing Reinhart. Reinhart's own result is that the features are model-specific and that classifiers do not transfer across registers. Muñoz-Ortiz measured models released before 2024 without instruction tuning. [official]

The skill does not present marker density as proof of authorship. Its authorship sentences deny it. The residual risk is presentation: points, a density percentage, "Strong ... tells" and "reads AI-shaped" together read as a verdict to someone who skips the disclaimer. [inference]

One further tension to record. The Wikipedia page's opening says "Please do not merely treat these signs as the problems to be fixed; that could just make detection harder" because the signs point to deeper problems. The skill uses the signs as an editing worklist. That is a different purpose and a legitimate one, but the skill already handles the deeper problems for the source-related markers ("verification needed", not "rewrite needed"), and it should say that it does the same for unsupported claims in every channel. [inference]

### Recommendation for the project

- Keep the two "not proof of authorship" sentences. Copy the `SKILL.md` sentence into `assets/detection-report-template.txt` so it travels with any density figure.
- Add to Detection Notes: markers occur in human text, formal and non-native English are likelier to be flagged, detectors and human judges have both been shown to fail, and model styles change by version.
- Reword "Strong AI-writing tells" to "Patterns that read as generic when repeated" and "Why it reads AI-shaped" to "Why it weakens the text". Every fix in the file is a plain editing fix, so this loses nothing.
- Qualify the cluster rule: "supported for vocabulary in academic papers; a heuristic elsewhere".
